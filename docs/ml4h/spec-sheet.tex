\title{AfyaCore: On-Device Speech-to-Structured Clinical Records for
Health Facilities Without Reliable Connectivity}

\begin{abstract}
AfyaCore is an offline-first web application that turns a spoken consultation
into a structured, encrypted clinical record on the clinician's own phone, for
primary-care facilities in sub-Saharan Africa. Speech recognition (Whisper
base) and de-identification (OpenMed PII) run on the device; nothing leaves it
unless the facility configures synchronisation. A deterministic extractor
turns the transcript into typed fields, and nothing machine-derived enters the
record until a clinician confirms it beside its source phrase. The system is
functional end to end and technically validated, including field accuracy
measured from speech through the exact model files the phone runs; it has not
yet been evaluated with clinicians.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Primary-care facilities across sub-Saharan Africa record consultations on
paper: written once, hard to find at the next visit, and reaching national
reporting as a hand-tallied monthly aggregate. Digitisation fails on
constraints of the setting: intermittent connectivity, low-end Android
hardware, and staff who cannot spend a consultation typing. AfyaCore is a
data-capture tool for nurses, midwives and clinical officers at health posts
and health centres, used during or just after a general primary-care
consultation. The clinician dictates in French or English; on-device speech
recognition produces a transcript, a deterministic extractor parses vital
signs, diagnosis and prescriptions into typed fields, and each field is shown
with its source phrase and confidence for the clinician to confirm. It does
not diagnose, triage or recommend treatment; vital-sign colouring is a fixed
published threshold table, not a model output. The device is assumed to be
shared, offline for long periods, and at risk of loss.

\section{Method}

\textbf{Architecture.} A single-page progressive web application with an
optional self-hosted synchronisation server. The initial blocking load is
154\,kB gzipped, a service worker precaches the shell, and the application is
fully functional with the network disabled.

\textbf{Speech recognition.} OpenAI Whisper \texttt{base} (72.6M parameters)
\citep{radford2022whisper}, exported to ONNX, quantised to int8 (81\,MB) and
run in a Web Worker through \texttt{transformers.js} on WebAssembly. Without
it the application falls back to the browser's Web Speech API, which sends
audio off the device, so dictation is then disabled behind an explicit
administrator acknowledgement.

\textbf{Extraction and review.} The transcript is parsed by a deterministic,
locale-aware rule system rather than a language model, because the failure
mode of a generative extractor is a plausible wrong number in a clinical field
with nothing downstream to catch it. Each value carries a plausibility range,
is dropped if outside it, and is confirmed individually beside the phrase it
came from; a later change makes it pending again. Because a correction lands
in a record that is already final, an unconfirmed value is also withheld from
the export and the monthly aggregate.

\textbf{De-identification and encryption.} Exports pass through a
deterministic stage that removes every identifier the device holds for the
patient, then an optional on-device neural stage (OpenMed PII French Clinical,
33M parameters, 70\,MB) \citep{openmed2025} for identifiers the roster has
never seen. At rest, the clinical stores are AES-GCM ciphertext under a
256-bit key wrapped per staff account by PBKDF2-SHA256 (600{,}000 iterations)
over that clinician's PIN, held in memory only for the session.

\textbf{Data.} A synthetic corpus of 22 cases, scored as text and again as
text-to-speech audio pushed through the same model files, extractor and scorer
the phone runs. Clinical-content retention is scored against gold
\texttt{CLINENTITY} annotations in the E3C corpus \citep{magnini2021e3c}.

\section{Results}

A functional end-to-end system at version 0.0.2, deployed publicly and
open-sourced under MIT. It has \emph{not} been piloted in a facility and no
clinician has used it in practice; the evidence below is technical
validation, which is what this stage supports.

The system carries 632 automated tests and a fourteen-step offline walk that
drives the production build through a real browser: it transcribes both
languages, disconnects, reloads, recreates the speech worker, transcribes
again and asserts identical transcripts. Nine country profiles and patient
instruction sheets in ten languages, including Kiswahili, Wolof, Hausa and
Malagasy, are shipped.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Measuring the pipeline, not its parts, found the defects.} An
extractor at 100\% and a recogniser with a published word error rate said
nothing about fields from speech until the two were scored together. That
cost 33 to 40 points of F1 and showed where: the recogniser writes a comma
where a full stop was spoken, so a diagnosis swallows the prescription after
it, and it spells drugs as it hears them (\emph{par assez tamol} for
\emph{parac\'etamol}). Each failure is now handled and pinned by a test.

\textbf{A threshold nobody chose is not a control.} The flag for uncertain
values was \texttt{confidence < 0.8}, and the extractor gives every diagnosis
and chief complaint exactly 0.8, so the two fields a recogniser mangles most
could never be flagged; a reviewer reading flagged rows first reached one
error in five. Likewise, confirmation before a draft becomes final was not a
complete rule, since a correction lands in a final record: an unconfirmed
diagnosis reached a research export and a district malaria count. Both were
found by measuring a control rather than reading it.

\textbf{The neural scrub is weakest where the tool is aimed, and it eats
diagnoses.} All three off-roster identifiers it missed were Malagasy: a French
model with an English WordPiece vocabulary fragments Malagasy proper nouns. On
real French text it cost 2.4\% of gold clinical entities before a guard was
added, including \emph{lymphome malin non hodgkinien}, because Hodgkin is a
surname. Neither finding was reachable on a corpus we wrote ourselves.

\textbf{Hindsight and next.} An external review found five release blockers,
each a place where documentation flattered behaviour; the worst was dictation
streaming audio to the browser vendor under a claim of no third-party call.
The system was built to a technical standard before it was put in front of a
clinician, which is the wrong order. Next is a formative study at one facility
and consented role-play recordings from intended users in place of synthetic
speech. Eight of ten translations are unreviewed by a speaker, the FHIR bundle
has not passed the official validator, and encryption at rest covers the
device but not the server.

\textbf{In-person demonstration.} The application installs from a URL on any
modern phone and runs entirely offline with a synthetic workspace, dictation
included, so it runs on attendees' own devices with no network and no real
patient data. Its evaluation also ships as a screen: an attendee runs the
speech model on a case and sees each field, whether it matched, whether it was
flagged, and how a correction changes the score.
